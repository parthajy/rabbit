# 4000 words client meeting transcript with more objections and back-and-forth.

Absolutely — below is a **fictional, realistic client meeting transcript** with **more objections, sharper back-and-forth, and a natural conversation flow**. I’ve kept it detailed and business-like, with the pitch, pushback, and negotiation all playing out in dialogue.

***

**Aditi Sharma:** Thanks for joining, Mr. Verma. I know this is the second conversation in a week, and I appreciate the time. I wanted this to be more of a working session than a polished pitch. We’ve got your feedback from **11 April 2026**, the internal notes from your team, and the questions you sent last night, so I thought we could go point by point.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good. Because I don’t want another glossy presentation that looks great until you ask how it actually works. We’ve seen enough of those. I need to know whether this proposal solves the real problems, what it costs, and what could go wrong.

**Aditi Sharma:** Completely fair. The proposal is for a six-month growth and conversion program focused on your consumer finance funnel. The objective is to improve qualified lead quality, reduce drop-off in the application process, and create a more predictable follow-up system for your sales and operations teams. Right now, the biggest bottlenecks appear to be lead quality, slow follow-up, and too much friction during application start.

**Rajiv Verma:** “Appear to be” is doing a lot of work there. I’d say they’re not just appearing. They’re real. We’re seeing the same issues in the dashboard every week, and the board has started asking why our lead volume is decent but our conversion rate is flat.

**Aditi Sharma:** Understood. The reason I used “appear” is because I don’t want to overstate the diagnosis before we’ve had a formal audit. But yes, based on the numbers your team shared, the issues are clear. For example, your current landing page completion rate is around **38%**, and users are dropping off most often at the document upload stage.

**Rajiv Verma:** That’s one of the things I wanted to challenge. You’re assuming the problem is friction. What if the issue is trust? People may be hesitant to submit documents if they don’t fully believe the offer is legitimate.

**Aditi Sharma:** That’s possible, and honestly, it’s probably part of the issue. It’s rarely one thing. That’s why the proposal includes both UX changes and trust-building changes: clearer copy, stronger social proof, tighter FAQ placement, and faster follow-up. If a user feels uncertain and then hears back late, the uncertainty gets worse.

**Rajiv Verma:** Okay, but you still haven’t told me why your approach is better than what we’re doing in-house. We already have a team. They know the product and the customer. Why do we need an outside partner?

**Aditi Sharma:** Because your internal team is already overloaded and operating in pieces. The marketing team is focused on campaign delivery, sales is focused on closing, and operations is focused on compliance and support. What’s missing is a single system that connects those stages. Our role is to design that system, manage the handoffs, and measure where the pipeline leaks. The result should be better conversion without adding chaos to your team’s workload.

**Rajiv Verma:** That sounds nice, but everyone says they “design systems.” What does that actually mean in practice?

**Aditi Sharma:** In practice, it means we’d map the funnel end-to-end. We’d audit the current traffic sources, create segment-specific campaigns, improve the landing page structure, define lead scoring logic, and set up follow-up triggers in the CRM. Then we’d monitor performance weekly and adjust. We’re not just offering ad buying or content. We’re offering a conversion framework.

**Rajiv Verma:** Fine. Let’s talk money. Your proposal says **₹24 lakh** for six months. That’s not trivial. What exactly is included, and what isn’t?

**Aditi Sharma:** Included: strategy, execution planning, performance oversight, creative direction, landing page optimization guidance, CRM workflow recommendations, weekly reporting, and monthly review sessions. Not included: media spend, major product development, or large-scale engineering changes. We can recommend changes to your forms or workflows, but your internal team would implement them unless we agree otherwise.

**Rajiv Verma:** So I’m paying a fairly significant fee, but I still need my own people to execute half of the changes. That’s what some would call a soft outsourcing model.

**Aditi Sharma:** I’d call it a collaborative model. If we overpromise and say we’ll do everything, the project becomes slower and more expensive. We’d rather stay inside the things we can actually influence.

**Rajiv Verma:** Hmm. That’s honest, at least. But I still need to understand how you expect the economics to improve. Right now our cost per qualified lead is around **₹1,840**, and our cost per acquisition is near **₹12,600**. What’s the realistic improvement?

**Aditi Sharma:** In a six-month window, our target would be a **15% to 20%** reduction in cost per acquisition and a **20% lift** in qualified lead quality. That doesn’t mean we’ll magically cut acquisition costs in half. It means the same spend should produce better downstream results. If we do this well, you should also see fewer unqualified leads wasting sales time.

**Rajiv Verma:** “Should” again. You’re very careful with language.

**Aditi Sharma:** I have to be. I don’t want to sell certainty where there isn’t any. If a campaign agency promises perfect results, that’s usually the first sign they don’t understand the market.

**Rajiv Verma:** Fair enough. But I need stronger proof than that. Have you done this for a financial product before? One that had compliance constraints, approval delays, and a sales team that wants leads yesterday?

**Aditi Sharma:** Yes, though I can’t disclose client names without permission. We’ve worked on regulated customer journeys before, and the pattern is similar: trust, timing, and friction are the key variables. In one recent case, by simplifying the application flow and improving follow-up timing, we reduced drop-off by **18%** over about four months. It wasn’t magic. It was a combination of message clarity and process discipline.

**Rajiv Verma:** Four months is a long time. We need movement sooner than that.

**Aditi Sharma:** The first signals come sooner. We’d expect early indicators within **2 to 3 weeks** of launch: better click-through rates, improved form starts, and fewer exits on the landing page. The harder metrics like acquisition cost and completed applications take longer to stabilize.

**Rajiv Verma:** That’s one of my concerns. We’ve been burned by agencies claiming “quick wins” that were really just vanity metrics. What I need are the numbers that matter to the board.

**Aditi Sharma:** Understood. We would focus on four board-level metrics: qualified lead rate, application completion rate, cost per acquisition, and conversion from application to approval. We’d also show segment performance so you can see whether salaried professionals, repeat borrowers, or small business owners respond differently.

**Rajiv Verma:** Segment performance is important. The current team treats everyone too much like one blob. But I want to push on this. Your plan seems to assume segmentation alone will fix everything. What if the fundamental offer is the issue? What if people don’t like the product terms?

**Aditi Sharma:** Then we’ll learn that quickly. One of the reasons for testing multiple segments and messages is to determine whether the issue is the offer or the communication around it. If one segment converts better with the same offer, the problem is likely messaging or workflow. If none of them convert well, then yes, we need to revisit the product itself. The proposal is designed to reveal that.

**Rajiv Verma:** Okay. That’s more reasonable. Another issue: your plan mentions landing page optimization. Our product and engineering team has very little time. If they can’t implement changes fast, what happens?

**Aditi Sharma:** Then we prioritize the highest-impact changes first. We’re not proposing a complete rebuild. The initial recommendations would focus on copy, form length, CTA placement, FAQ visibility, and trust signals. Those are mostly low-lift. If deeper changes are needed later, we can plan them for a second phase.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good, because I don’t want a consulting project that creates a queue of engineering work we never finish.

**Aditi Sharma:** Completely agreed. That’s one of the risks we try to avoid. We’ve seen projects where the agency throws recommendations over the wall and then disappears. That’s not useful.

**Rajiv Verma:** Let me ask a tougher question. Why is your fee so much higher than the **₹18 lakh** quote we got from another agency? On paper, they’re offering something similar.

**Aditi Sharma:** Usually, when a proposal is much cheaper, one of two things is happening. Either the scope is thinner than it looks, or the team is not budgeting enough time for optimization. In the first month alone, we’ll spend time on audit, reporting setup, workflow design, and copy improvements. If another agency is doing that at a much lower cost, I’d ask what they’re leaving out. It may be media management, it may be reporting depth, or it may be that they’re planning to run a few campaigns and call it strategy.

**Rajiv Verma:** So your answer is basically “we’re not the cheap option because we actually intend to work.”

**Aditi Sharma:** In a sense, yes.

**Rajiv Verma:** Fair. That’s at least refreshingly unflattering.

**Aditi Sharma:** I do my best.

**Rajiv Verma:** Now, what happens if the board cuts the budget after two months? Because that’s not impossible. They are already nervous about the quarter.

**Aditi Sharma:** Then the program becomes less effective. Most of the learning happens in the first **6 to 8 weeks**, but the optimization cycle needs time. If funding drops early, we may be able to preserve some learnings, but the full outcome would be weaker. That’s why I’d recommend securing approval for at least the first three months upfront.

**Rajiv Verma:** And the payment structure?

**Aditi Sharma:** We proposed **40% upfront**, **30% after the first optimization review on 20 May 2026**, and **30% after the July review on 31 July 2026**. If your finance team prefers, we can discuss a minor adjustment, but the core logic is to align payment with milestones rather than just calendar dates.

**Rajiv Verma:** I’m not a fan of heavy upfront fees.

**Aditi Sharma:** Understandable. But the upfront is tied to setup work that happens immediately. If you reduce it significantly, we’d need to reduce the scope or shift some deliverables later.

**Rajiv Verma:** Let’s not hide behind structure. What’s the lowest you can go without damaging the work?

**Aditi Sharma:** We could potentially lower the upfront a little if we move to a more conservative start, but I wouldn’t want to reduce overall value just to make the first invoice easier to swallow. If price flexibility is essential, we could explore a smaller pilot engagement of **3 months** first, then expand if the results justify it.

**Rajiv Verma:** That might actually be a better discussion. What would a pilot look like?

**Aditi Sharma:** A pilot would focus on one segment, probably salaried professionals or repeat borrowers. We’d optimize one funnel, one landing page, and one nurture path. Budget would be lower, but the learning would be narrower. The advantage is lower risk. The disadvantage is you don’t get the full system benefit right away.

**Rajiv Verma:** That’s probably what finance will ask for. They like pilots because they can say “yes” without committing emotionally.

**Aditi Sharma:** Makes sense. But a small pilot can also understate the value if we only optimize one part of the journey. If the board wants confidence, the full program is better. If they want reassurance, the pilot is safer.

**Rajiv Verma:** You’re giving me options, which I appreciate, but I need to know which option you actually recommend.

**Aditi Sharma:** I recommend the full six-month program if your internal teams can support the pace. If there’s too much internal resistance or budget uncertainty, then start with the pilot. I’d rather begin narrower than force a broad program that gets strangled by indecision.

**Rajiv Verma:** That’s sensible. Another concern: compliance. We’ve had some issues where marketing copy gets drafted, approved, and then someone from legal changes it at the last minute. That causes delays and annoyed people. How would you handle that?

**Aditi Sharma:** We’d build a pre-approved messaging framework. That means we work with compliance at the beginning, identify the language that can be reused, and create a library of safe claims, disclaimers, and approved terms. That way we reduce the number of one-off approvals. It won’t eliminate review, but it should reduce the back-and-forth.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good. I like reducing back-and-forth. Humans spend too much time pretending they like meetings about the same sentence.

**Aditi Sharma:** Completely. We’d also create a checklist for new creative so the team can self-audit before sending anything to legal. That saves a lot of time.

**Rajiv Verma:** Fine. Now tell me about the reporting. I want something I can put in front of leadership without having to translate it.

**Aditi Sharma:** We’ll send a weekly dashboard that includes spend, lead volume, qualified lead rate, application starts, completions, drop-off by stage, and cost per acquisition. In addition, once a month we’ll send a short narrative summary explaining what changed, what worked, what didn’t, and what we’re doing next.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good. I want narratives, not just charts. The CFO likes charts, but the CEO wants a story.

**Aditi Sharma:** That’s exactly the balance we aim for.

**Rajiv Verma:** Let’s revisit the implementation burden. You said you need one marketing point person, one CRM owner, one designer, and access to the sales head. How much time will that actually take?

**Aditi Sharma:** The first two weeks are heavier, because we’ll be gathering data and aligning on process. After that, maybe **4 to 5 hours a week** from the core team, mostly in review and approvals. It should be manageable unless meetings multiply like rabbits.

**Rajiv Verma:** Meetings do multiply like rabbits here. I’ll try to prevent that.

**Aditi Sharma:** Much appreciated.

**Rajiv Verma:** I’m still not fully sold on the fee. I can see the logic, but procurement will ask for a stronger rationale. If I bring them a proposal that says “pay more because we are thorough,” they’ll laugh me out of the room.

**Aditi Sharma:** Then don’t frame it that way. Frame it as reducing leakage in the funnel and improving downstream revenue. If we can improve conversion at even a modest level, the fee becomes easier to justify. For example, if the program helps generate just **150 additional qualified leads** over six months and improves completion enough to produce **25 to 30 extra conversions**, the economics begin to make sense quickly.

**Rajiv Verma:** That’s the kind of argument I can use. Still, I need to be careful. We have had vendors promise outcome numbers that looked impressive until we asked how they were calculated.

**Aditi Sharma:** Totally fair. We’d be transparent about the assumptions. We won’t promise exact results. We’ll show the base case, the conservative case, and the upside case. That way leadership can choose based on risk tolerance rather than optimism.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good. One final objection: your proposal seems very dependent on internal cooperation. What if my team is slower than expected? What if product doesn’t change the page fast enough or sales doesn’t follow up quickly enough?

**Aditi Sharma:** Then results will be weaker. I won’t pretend otherwise. This is a cross-functional improvement program. If one part of the chain is slow, the system slows. But that’s also why we propose weekly operating reviews. We can surface bottlenecks early and nudge the right people before too much time is lost.

**Rajiv Verma:** That’s honest. I can live with honest.

**Aditi Sharma:** Good, because I’d rather be useful than persuasive.

**Rajiv Verma:** That may be the most non-sales thing I’ve heard from a salesperson in a while.

**Aditi Sharma:** I’ll take that as a compliment too.

**Rajiv Verma:** You should. Okay, let’s summarize. I’m interested in the full six-month approach, but I want a revised version that shows either a lower-risk pilot or a more flexible pricing structure. I also want clearer language around assumptions, internal responsibilities, and what success looks like in the first **30 to 45 days**.

**Aditi Sharma:** That’s clear. I’ll send a revised proposal by **13 April 2026** with two options: the full engagement and a three-month pilot. I’ll include more detail on milestones, reporting, compliance handling, and resource needs. I’ll also add a tighter explanation of what happens in the first month so your team can see the actual sequence.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good. I’ll take that to internal review on **15 April 2026**. If the room is favorable, we can schedule a kickoff on **18 April 2026**. If not, we’ll probably ask for one more round of revision.

**Aditi Sharma:** That works. And if your finance team wants to discuss structure, I can join for a short call on **16 April 2026**.

**Rajiv Verma:** We may need that. Finance likes to ask questions that sound simple and then somehow become a two-hour philosophical discussion.

**Aditi Sharma:** I’m ready for that.

**Rajiv Verma:** Good. I appreciate the direct answers today. You didn’t oversell it, which is rare. I’m still cautious, but this is a more credible conversation than the usual pitch circus.

**Aditi Sharma:** Thank you, Mr. Verma. I’ll send the revised document tomorrow and keep the language as practical as possible.

**Rajiv Verma:** Perfect. Let’s keep this moving. If we can make the funnel less leaky and avoid wasting the sales team’s time, I’ll consider that a win.

**Aditi Sharma:** That’s the goal. Thank you again for the time.

**Rajiv Verma:** Thank you.
